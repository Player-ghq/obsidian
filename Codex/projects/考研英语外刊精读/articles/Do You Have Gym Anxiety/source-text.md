## Source Text

How to walk into the gym with confidence - and train without self-doubt.

Feeling nervous about the gym? You're not weak - and you're definitely not alone. Gym anxiety affects men at every level, from first-timers to experienced lifters. So, if walking into the weights room makes you hesitate, compare yourself to others, or question what you're doing, it's more common than you think.

For many men, it's not just about being new - it's about status, strength and self-image. How much am I lifting? Do I look in shape enough? Am I doing this right? What if I embarrass myself?

"These fears are deeply rooted," says Adam Tallamy, one of the UK's leading life coaches. "For men, feeling less strong, less capable or less 'manly' taps into something primal - hierarchy, competition, survival. It's not weakness. It's human nature."

## Why the gym can feel intimidating for men

Exercise is supposed to reduce anxiety - but it can also create it. If you feel too anxious to go to the gym, you avoid it. If you do go and feel anxious, you start questioning why. It's a vicious circle.

"The gym can seem intimidating, especially from the outside looking in," says Maxwell Thompson, Academy Trainer and Personal Trainer at David Lloyd Clubs.

"Heavy weights banging, men grunting, ripped guys taking selfies or filming a new PB - without context, it's easy to feel out of place. But like anything new, it's okay to be a beginner."

"Walking into a room full of strangers and performing in an open space can make you feel exposed," adds Tallamy. "Add comparison and self-judgement, and suddenly every rep feels like it's being assessed."

For many men, this anxiety centres around feeling smaller or weaker than others, lifting less weight, lacking confidence in technique, or worrying about how they look - especially around more experienced lifters or attractive women.

"If you've never been properly taught how to lift, technique insecurity can also be huge," says Tallamy. "Men often feel they should already know how to lift - so they suffer in silence, worried about being called out." The reality? That almost never happens - but the fear still feels real.

The good news is that it's normal to feel nervous - but like a muscle, you can train yourself to outsmart it. Here's how to overcome some common scenarios.

## Am I too unfit for the gym?

"I hear this all the time - and there's no perfect moment to start. Wait for the 'right time', and you'll never go," says Thompson.

"Short, regular visits are one of the fastest ways to build confidence. Cardio machines are a great place to begin - they're simple, effective, and work for everyone. Don't worry about what anyone else is doing, and don't feel pressure to use unfamiliar kit."

"And if the weight area feels intimidating, resistance machines are a good option," explains Thompson. "Most come with clear instructions showing how to use them and which muscles they target. Or why not grab a couple of dumbbells and head to a quieter corner, where you can stick to a simple full-body workout using dumbbells and bodyweight? Focus on showing up first - performance comes later."

Tallamy agrees: "Every visit is progress. Get through those first few sessions, build the habit, and the fear drops quickly. It's mostly a mental barrier."

## How do I know what to do in the gym?

Walking in without direction is one of the biggest confidence killers.

"Without a plan, men tend to drift, compare themselves to others, and second-guess what they're doing - which can quickly lead to feeling lost or self-conscious," says Thompson.

"Having a simple plan gives you direction, helps you track progress, and builds confidence. One of my go-tos is to start with a brief cardio warm-up, then choose four to six exercises and perform them as a circuit - 45 seconds on, 15 seconds off. It's a simple way to get familiar with the gym and its equipment."

Tallamy adds, "Build the habit, and things get easier. For busy men, blocking out fixed time in your calendar each week can be a game-changer. Treat training like a work meeting - a lunch break or a morning ritual. Make it non-negotiable."

## What should I wear?

It's not about looking flashy - it's about feeling comfortable and capable.

"If you're constantly adjusting your clothes or feeling self-conscious, it affects how you train," says Tallamy.

"Choose activewear that's breathable, fits well, and lets you move freely," says Thompson. "You don't need anything complicated - just something you feel good in - don't fall into the trap of thinking you need the latest matching set to get started."

And if changing rooms feels intimidating, you're not alone. "Many men feel self-conscious in that environment," says Tallamy. "If it helps, arrive ready to train and keep things simple."

## Will listening to music distract me?

Music can be a powerful way to focus - not distract. "We all have that playlist that fires us up," says Thompson. "Make sure your music matches the session. Use high-energy tracks for the start of your session, and for your lifting or cardio, then slow things down as you finish."

The right playlist can help block out distractions, reduce self-consciousness, and keep you focused on your workout.

"You can also tailor your audio to your goal," Thompson adds. "Create playlists for different sessions - or switch to a podcast if you want something more steady and engaging."

## What if I'm not as big or in as good shape as the other guys?

"For men feeling less than, threatened by or not manly enough based on how much other guys are lifting, or how big they look, it may be worth training with a mate. For men, this is quite a primal thing and links back to survival of the fittest and strongest - but training doesn't have to be a solo battle," says Tallamy. "Going with a friend not only boosts accountability, but reminds you that everyone started somewhere - you aren't alone."

Thompson adds: "The main thing to remember is that everyone is there for themselves, and if there is one thing the gym community thrives on, it's seeing others succeed. So if you're ever unsure, I'd advise you to always go to a trainer - or a fellow gym goer and ask for their help. Even small interactions can make the gym feel more human."

## Will everyone stare at me?

"If you give yourself permission to show up without being perfect, you'll keep showing up," says Tallamy.

You don't need flawless technique, huge dumbbells, or a shredded physique to belong in the gym - you need consistency. Regular gym-goers report far higher confidence than those who train sporadically. Confidence isn't something you wait for - you build it.

"Like anything in life, the more familiar we are with something, the more confident we feel approaching it. The gym is no different," Thompson adds. "Once you find a routine that fits your lifestyle and stick to it - whether 3, 4, or 5 times a week, for 30, 45, or 60 minutes - consistency allows you to improve your skills, try new things, and build confidence. Eventually, you'll look back and wonder what had you so worried in the first place."

## Do I need a coach?

"Some anxiety runs deeper," says Tallamy. "Working with a coach or therapist can completely change how you see yourself - for many men, gym anxiety is tied to deeper pressures around masculinity, performance, and the fear of not being good enough. You can totally change how you feel about almost anything if you put in the time and find the right assistance."

So it's worth remembering that support isn't weakness. It's a strategy.

Plus a "major source of anxiety comes from worrying about 'doing it wrong'," says Thompson. Investing in a coach can be transformative - not only for learning correct technique, but also for building confidence and removing uncertainty.
